The four ways to split a game

One setting, four answers. Four sample players, one game; the number on the right is that player’s total at the buzzer.

Even

Everybody as close to the same number of minutes as the clock allows, and the default. Where the clock will not divide evenly, somebody plays one stint fewer. The plan records who, and the season further down pays it back.

By hand

20 16 12 12

You set each player’s total yourself and the plan hits it. The totals always add up to the whole game, so giving one player more takes it from the others in front of you rather than by accident. Lock the two or three you care about and leave the rest; an unlocked total is free to move.

Closers

Even minutes for most of the game, then a group you name stays on to finish, in the shaded window on every track above. The minutes still come out level, because the closers give the time back earlier.

Platoon

16 16 16 16

Two or three fixed fives that swap as whole units. It is the easiest thing to run from the bench, and good for the youngest ages. The minutes only come out even if the groups themselves are even, so here the roster does the work rather than the solver.

Where your strongest lineups fall

How long everybody plays and who they play beside are two different questions. This is the second one.

Give each player a level on the Team page, once a season rather than once a game, and Benchcard keeps the fives from tipping. The share of the minutes is still worked out without the levels: they change who shares the floor, not how long anyone is on it. Then, beside each game, pick where the strong lineups land:

  • Steady — every stint about as strong as every other. Nobody is stranded with a five that cannot compete.
  • Start strong — your best five open the game. Under even minutes that means lighter stints at the end; those minutes have to go somewhere.
  • Finish strong — the reverse, saving the strongest five for the last stretch.
  • Both ends — strong to open and strong to close, softer in the middle. Pick this one to get developing players on the floor in the stretch where the game is usually already decided.

The whole feature is off until you use it: everyone starts on the middle level, every five is worth the same, and the plan is the plan you would have got anyway.

The seven rules

Rules are things that have to be true. Everything else bends to fit, and the plan holds all of them at once.

  • Minimum and Cap — at least, or at most, a number of minutes for one player. “At least 10” guarantees playing time; “capped at 12” holds somebody back after an illness or a long week.
  • Starting five — who is on the floor at tip-off.
  • Starts the last period — who is on when the last period begins (its first stint only; overtime does not count). To keep a group on at the end, use Closers.
  • Together — two players who share the floor as much as the plan allows. It is a preference the plan maximizes by default, and you can promote it to a hard requirement at a cost in evenness.
  • Apart — two players never on the floor at the same time, and the only rule most coaches set for reasons that have nothing to do with basketball.
  • One of two on — a pair you never want resting at the same time. Name your two ball handlers, or your two bigs, and one of them is on the floor every stint. It is the mirror of Apart: that one is about the floor, this one is about the bench.
  • Plays at most N in a row — the most stints anyone may play back to back, so nobody is left out there for a whole half.
  • Sits at most N in a row — the most stints anyone may sit back to back, so nobody watches from the bench for a whole half. When the roster is large, holding it can take more changes at a break than you allow, and the plan says so.

When two rules cannot both hold, the plan says so in a note beneath it rather than quietly dropping one, naming the rule it could not honor alongside everything else that was asked of it.

A league minimum for the whole team

One number, set once, that reaches every available player in every game.

If your league says everyone plays a minimum, Settings holds one number for the whole team, Everyone plays at least … min, so you are not adding a rule for each player before each game. It composes with the per-player rules above: a minimum you set on one player wins when it is higher, and a cap you set wins when it is lower, so a cap under a league rule holds instead of turning into an error nobody asked for.

Worth setting on the day you install this, before the roster is finished.

Changing the plan mid-game

Foul trouble, a twisted ankle, a kid who is having a miserable afternoon. The card is a plan, not a contract.

  • This stint — a one-off swap for the stint on the floor now. Benchcard then re-plans the rest of the game so minutes stay even, so your printed card is out of date from here on: follow bench mode.
  • Rest of game — the swap holds from here to the buzzer. Use it when somebody has gone off for the afternoon and you already know who is taking their place.
  • Sit for the rest — you know who is coming off and not who is replacing them. Benchcard picks, spreading the freed minutes over whoever is furthest behind rather than handing them all to one player.

Every one of them is undoable from the toast that follows. The card in your pocket stays the record of what was planned; the app knows what actually happened.

Overtime

A tied game goes on. On the last stint in bench mode, tap Tied? Add overtime, set the minutes and choose who plays it:

  • Next rotation — the plan carries on as if the game were longer.
  • Even out — the extra minutes go first to whoever has played least.
  • Keep this five — the five on the floor now play all of it.
  • Strongest five — your highest-level players at the gym play all of it.

Then tap Add overtime. One Undo takes it back. The overtime travels in the hand-off link, and the card prints it as OT.

Overtime is split into stints as long as the regular ones. A short leftover is folded into the stint before it.

The season

The part a phone is better at than a parent under pressure: remembering, in March, who was short in November.

Turn Even out the season so far on and the next plan opens each player ahead or behind by how far they are off their share of everything played to date. It moves nobody more than two stints either way, and never over a cap or a locked number. The targets it handed the solver are printed underneath the switch, so a number never moves without saying why.

A tournament day is the same machinery on a shorter clock. Add a second game and it copies the format, who is at the gym and your rules; only the opponent and the tip-off are new. The day balances across every game by default, so a player who sat more in game one gets it back in game two.

The card, and its two sizes

Three blocks of a printed card, at the size they print.

Two sizes, under Size:

  • Pocket size — 3.45 × 5 in, one column. Folds into a shirt pocket and survives a gym.
  • Half sheet — 8 × 5.1 in, two columns. Bigger type at arm’s length, and the second column is what keeps a long game on one card instead of two.

Both print from the browser with no app and no account, and both are legible from a clipboard in a dim gym.

Handing a game to an assistant

An assistant can run the subs from their own phone. On the game screen, tap the share icon, then Hand off. The sheet shows a code and a Share link button; they scan the code or open the link and the game is on their phone, ready for bench mode.

What travels is one game: its settings, the team name and color, the rotation, and where the game stands, including the stints already played. For each player it carries the card name, jersey number and skill tier. Full names stay behind. It is a copy, so the two phones are not linked afterward: a change on one does not show up on the other.

Where your team is stored

Your roster never leaves your device. Benchcard runs in your browser. Your roster, your games and your rules are stored on the device you typed them into. There is no account and no server holding your team, so clearing your browser data clears it too. The site counts anonymous usage (page views, and which features get used) so I can see what is worth building next; those are counters only, never a name, a team or an opponent. A hand-off link carries one game, with each player’s card name, number and skill tier, to whoever you send it to; the game rides after the #, which a browser never sends, so no Benchcard server sees it.

Settings can write a backup file with your roster, your rules and your season in it, and read one back in the same panel. Worth doing once at the start of a season, and again any time you would hate to lose the season you have built up.
