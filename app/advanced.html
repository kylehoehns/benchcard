The four ways to split a game

One setting with four choices. Four sample players in one game. The number on the right is each player’s minutes at the end.

Even

16 16 16 16

Everybody plays as close to the same minutes as the clock allows. This is the default. When the minutes do not divide evenly, some kids play one turn less (a turn is the time between two subs). The plan keeps track of who, and the season, further down, can make it up to them.

By hand

20 16 12 12

You set each player’s minutes yourself, and the plan follows them. The totals always add up to the whole game, so giving one kid more takes minutes from someone else, and you see it happen. Lock the two or three totals you care about. The ones you do not lock can move to make room.

Even minutes for most of the game, then a group you pick plays the end, the shaded part of each row above. Minutes still come out even, because the closers sit more earlier in the game.

Platoon

16 16 16 16

Two or three set groups of five that always go in and come out together. It is the easiest to run from the bench and works well for the youngest kids. Minutes only come out even if the groups are even, so here it is up to how you split the roster, not Benchcard.

Where your strongest lineups fall

How long everybody plays and who they play beside are two different questions. This is the second one.

Give each player a level on the Team page. You do it once a season, not every game, and Benchcard keeps each five evenly matched. Levels do not change how many minutes the plan aims to give each kid. They change who plays together. Then, for each game, pick when your strongest lineups play:

  • Steady — every lineup about as strong as every other. No kid gets stuck in a lineup that cannot compete.
  • Start strong — your best five open the game. With even minutes, that means weaker lineups at the end, because those minutes have to go somewhere.
  • Finish strong — the reverse, saving the strongest five for the last stretch.
  • Both ends — strong to open and strong to close, softer in the middle. Pick this one to get developing players on the floor in the stretch where the game is usually already decided.

If you never set levels, nothing changes. Everyone starts on the middle level, and the plan comes out the same as before.

The seven rules

A rule is something the plan must do. Everything else fits around your rules, and the plan follows all of them at once.

  • Plays at least and Plays at most — the fewest or the most minutes one player gets. “At least 10” guarantees playing time; “at most 12” holds a kid back after an illness or a long week.
  • Starting five — who is on the floor at tip-off.
  • Starts the last period — who is on when the last period begins (the first lineup only; overtime does not count). To keep a group on at the end, use Closers.
  • Together — two players who share the floor. You set it on each Together rule: As much as possible, the default, pairs them as often as the plan can. Always makes it a must, but minutes may come out less even.
  • Apart — two players never on the floor at the same time. It is the only rule most coaches set for reasons that have nothing to do with basketball.
  • One of two on — a pair you never want resting at the same time. Name your two ball handlers, or your two bigs, and one of them is always on the floor. It is the mirror of Apart: that one is about the floor, this one is about the bench.
  • Plays at most … in a row — the most turns on the floor in a row a kid can take before a rest. A break between periods is not a rest. A kid who plays the end of one period and the start of the next has played two in a row. Set it to 1 and all five change at every sub. If you only sub between periods, each turn is a whole period. The count is then in quarters (or halves, or periods).
  • Sits at most … in a row — the most turns in a row a kid can sit on the bench. A break between periods does not reset the count. With a big roster, this can need more players to change at once than you allow. The plan tells you when that happens.

When two rules cannot both be met, a note under the plan says so. It names the rule it could not follow, so nothing is dropped without telling you.

A league minimum for the whole team

One number, set once, that reaches every available player in every game.

If your league has a minimum playing time, set it once in Settings with Everyone plays at least … min. Then you do not add a rule for each player before each game. It works alongside the rules above. A Plays at least rule on one player wins when it is higher. A Plays at most rule wins when it is lower, so a kid you are holding back still gets held back, with no error.

The minimum can count in minutes, as a share of the game, in quarters of time, or in whole quarters. A whole quarter is a quarter played start to finish. A kid owed two whole quarters plays two of them, not the same minutes pieced together from parts of four. If the whole quarters you ask for cannot fit in the game, the plan will not build and shows you the numbers.

Everyone plays in, also in Settings, makes sure every kid gets on the floor in each half, or in each period. This can need more players to change at once than your limit allows, and the plan tells you how many. It also tells you when a half or period has too few subs to get everyone in.

Set it on your first day, before you finish your roster.

Changing the plan mid-game

Foul trouble or a twisted ankle can change your game. The card is a plan, and you can change it.

  • For now — a swap for the current group only, until the next sub. Benchcard then changes the rest of the plan to keep minutes even, so your printed card no longer matches. Follow bench mode from here.
  • Takes their minutes — the swap lasts until the end of the game. Use it when a kid is done for the day and you know who takes their place.
  • Sits the rest of the game — you know who is coming off but not who replaces them. Benchcard picks, and shares the extra minutes among the kids who have played least instead of giving them all to one player.

Each one can be undone from the message that pops up after it. The card in your pocket shows what you planned, and the app keeps track of what happened.

Overtime

A tied game goes on. Near the end of the game, on the bench screen, tap Tied? Add overtime. One tap adds it, with the minutes you used last time and Next rotation. To set the minutes or choose who plays it, tap Change next to it first:

  • Next rotation — the plan carries on as if the game were longer.
  • Even out — the extra minutes go first to whoever has played least.
  • Keep this five — the five on the floor now play all of it.
  • Strongest five — your highest-level players at the gym play all of it.

In the Change sheet, tap Add overtime to add it. One Undo takes it back. A hand-off link includes the overtime, and the card prints it as OT.

Overtime is split into turns as long as the regular ones. If a few minutes are left over, they are added to the turn before.

The season

A phone is better than a busy parent at this part: remembering in March who played less in November.

Pick The season so far under Make up minutes from. The next plan then gives a little more time to kids who are behind for the season. It gives a little less to kids who are ahead. It moves anyone by a sub or two at most, and never past a Plays at most limit or minutes you locked by hand. The changes are listed under the choice, so you can always see why someone's minutes moved.

A tournament day works the same way, over one day. Add a second game and it copies the format, who is at the gym and your rules; only the opponent and the tip-off are new. From the second game on, minutes even out across the whole day. A kid who sat more in game one plays more in game two.

The card, and its two sizes

Three blocks of a printed card, at the size they print.

Two sizes, under Size:

  • Pocket size — 3.45 × 5 in, one column. Folds into a shirt pocket and survives a gym.
  • Half sheet — 8 × 5.1 in, two columns. Bigger type at arm’s length, and the second column is what keeps a long game on one card instead of two.

Both print straight from your browser, with nothing to install and no account. Both are easy to read on a clipboard in a dim gym.

Handing a game to an assistant

An assistant can run the subs from their own phone. On the game screen, tap the share icon, then Hand off. The sheet shows a code and a Share link button. They scan the code or open the link, and the game is on their phone, ready for bench mode.

What travels is one game: its settings, the team name and color, the rotation, and where the game stands, including the part already played. For each player it carries the card name, jersey number and level. Full names stay behind. It is a copy, so the two phones are not linked afterward: a change on one does not show up on the other.

Where your team is stored

Your roster never leaves your device. Benchcard runs in your browser. Your roster, your games and your rules are stored on the device you typed them into. There is no account and no server holding your team, so clearing your browser data clears it too. The site counts anonymous usage, like page views and which features get used, so I can see what is worth building next. Those are counters only, never a name, a team or an opponent. A hand-off link carries one game to whoever you send it to, with each player’s card name, number and level. The game is stored after the # in the link. Browsers never send that part to a server, so no Benchcard server sees it.

In Settings you can save a backup file with your roster, your rules and your season, and restore it in the same place. Do it once at the start of the season, and again whenever you would hate to lose what you have.
